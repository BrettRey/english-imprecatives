Section~S1 gives the query inventory, Section~S2 the illustrative \mention{bless} comparison, and Sections~S3--S9 the corpus inventories and detailed audits. \section{Corpus query strings and adjudication protocol}\label{supp:queries}

Table~\ref{tab:appendix-queries} lists all Mark Davies queries (TV Corpus 325M; GloWbE 1.9B; LIST view). Tier~1 scanned all 13 heads and 6 primary interjections before closed-class NP starters (CABNC, OANC) and any NP-starting CLAWS5 tag (\texttt{PNP}, \texttt{AT0}, \texttt{DPS}, \texttt{DT0}, \texttt{NP0}, \texttt{NN1}, \texttt{NN2}, \texttt{PNI}, \texttt{CRD}) in BNC Baby XML.

\section{Tier-1 coding, inventory and coverage checks}\label{supp:tier1}

Routine \mention{bless you} takes priority over \mention{you}, and body-part formulae over full NPs. Optatives with overt \mention{God} are counted separately.

Transcriber capitalization differs across the compared uses. None of 80 verified subjectless tokens in CABNC has a capital between head and NP, against 9 of 23 primary-interjection sequences (\mention{Ouch My foot}) and 4 of 7 standalone \mention{damn} or \mention{fuck} tokens (\mention{Fuck. That would have been amusing}). The coding excluded standalone interjections before a new clause, and capitals were one rough cue to such boundaries (the article's methods section), so the comparison isn't fully independent of the coding.

The broader BNC Baby search recovered three bare nouns or proper names that the closed-class searches miss (\mention{Damn McIllvanney}, \mention{Oh, screw Jesus Christ}, \mention{night, bless mummy and dad}). It also exposed an ambiguity: \mention{Damn DTs.} is either a subjectless imprecative or prenominal \mention{damn} (compare \mention{Damn rubbish.}; \citealp[35 n.\ 29]{portner2026}). That ambiguity is why the large-corpus queries require an overt determinative, genitive pronoun or accusative pronoun.

Tier~1 also shows regional skew, though the registers differ too much for a controlled UK--US comparison. \mention{Sod} and \mention{bugger} each occur 20 times in the British data and once in the American. Overt \mention{God} already concentrates on the theological heads: it occurs in 20 of 102 \mention{bless} tokens and 3 of 36 \mention{damn} tokens (all \mention{God damn it}), and in none of 105 tokens of the other seven attested heads.

The peripheral-head search ranked clause-initial bare words before an accusative pronoun in BNC Baby by the log-likelihood ratio $G^2$ \citep{dunning1993}. This was an exploratory search for candidates outside the inherited inventory.

Apart from \mention{thank} and \mention{excuse}, ordinary imperatives and discourse particles, \mention{bless} ranks highest among imprecative and benedictive heads (20 tokens in 8 files), beside \mention{goodness me!}. Searching for heads proposed in the literature turns up peripheral British \mention{blow} (\mention{ooh, blow this for a lark}, \mention{blow me!}), \mention{stuff} (\mention{stuff it!}, \mention{stuff this!}) and \mention{stone} (\mention{stone the crows}). The thirteen heads are a sampling frame, not a closed inventory. Of its 51 hits with a determinative or genitive, 36 are imprecatives (\mention{Darn that felony!}), 4 of them with \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{darn the rains for messing with our social scene}), and 8 are the mending verb. \mention{Dang} mostly doesn't: 14 of its 20 hits are \mention{dang} before a clause (\mention{dang that list is long!}), and only 5 are imprecatives (\mention{Dang those puppy eyes}). With a pronoun target, separating targeted curses from bleached subjectless oaths on \mention{me} (matching how bleached \mention{Damn it!} and \mention{Fuck it!} enter the subjectless pattern) clarifies the split: \mention{darn} has 9 targeted curses and 1 bleached \mention{darn me} in 13 hits (10 of 13), \mention{drat} has 2 targeted curses in 2 hits (\mention{Niall, drat him, was no help}), and \mention{dang} has no targeted curses but 2 bleached oaths (\mention{Well dang me sideways}; 2 of 11, alongside 7 mentions of the song title \emph{Dang Me} and 2 other hits). In the 100-line \mention{damn} samples, 53 full-NP hits are imprecatives (and 20 are \mention{damn} before a clause, as in \mention{damn that meat was tender}), while 55 pronoun hits are targeted curses and 9 are bleached \mention{damn me} (\mention{if}) oaths (64 of 100 subjectless). \mention{Dang} thus shows formulaic subjectless uses (\mention{Well dang me sideways}, \mention{God dang it}) and five open-NP imprecatives, but its only two subjectless pronoun tokens are bleached oaths, too few to show that targeted pronoun curses are unavailable.

Second, two distinct \mention{be}+participle patterns diverge for the minced forms: the bare-subjunctive \mention{be} + participle dismissal is barely attested (a single \mention{Rules be darned}; the article's bare-subjunctive results), whereas the finite oath \mention{I'll be darned!} (and \mention{be darned if}) accounts for 99 of 100 sampled \mention{be darned} lines, mirroring finite \mention{I'll be damned!} rather than the bare-subjunctive dismissal.

Third, outside the pattern the minced forms share \mention{damn}'s uses as attributive modifier of a noun (\mention{that darn donkey}) and as modifier of an adjective (\mention{pretty darn good}; Table~\ref{tab:minced}), just as they copy fixed formulae inside it (\mention{God dang it}). Even \mention{dang}, which seldom occurs in the pattern, modifies the adjective in 90 of 100 sampled \mention{dang} + adjective lines. Because the correspondence holds outside the pattern too, the pattern alone doesn't account for it; both lexical entries and construction networks can state such a correspondence (the article's comparison of analytical accounts).

Fourth, the euphemisms of \mention{fuck} occur in the pattern in subtitles. In a 100-line sample of the 223 hits for \mention{frak}, \mention{fudge}, \mention{eff}, \mention{feck}, \mention{frig} and \mention{frick} + pronoun or determinative, 68 are subjectless imprecatives (\ref{ex:supp-euphemisms}), though \mention{frak} supplies 40 of them, all but one from \emph{Battlestar Galactica} and its spin-off \emph{Caprica}. Most of the rest are the ordinary verb \mention{fudge} \enquote{falsify} (\mention{fudge the numbers}).

\section{Spoken BNC2014 calibration}\label{supp:speech}

To check the subtitle distributions against unscripted speech, I repeated the core searches in Spoken BNC2014.

First, the living heads dominate: \mention{bless} (502 hits), \mention{fuck} (457), \mention{damn} (167), \mention{screw} (50), \mention{bugger} (27) and \mention{sod} (26). Auditing 100 of 237 lines with a determinative or genitive verifies 48 direct tokens, mostly demonstratives (\mention{sod this for a lark}) and \mention{fuck my life}, with five open full NPs (\mention{Fuck this boat club}, \mention{damn those vampires}, \mention{bless that poor girl who was acting}). The archaic and minced heads barely occur: of 22 hits, 4 are imprecatives (\mention{dash it all}, \mention{curse the world}, and \mention{darn it} twice), and there's a single \mention{oh confound it}.

Second, none of the 11 hits for the euphemisms of \mention{fuck} is an imprecative (9 are literal \mention{fudge}), although \mention{fuck it} (138) and \mention{fuck you} (94) are frequent. Their subtitle use may reflect broadcast censorship and scripted invention, which this data can't separate.

Third, overt \mention{God} again goes with the theological heads: 10 \mention{god damn it} and 5 verified \mention{god bless} optatives, and no secular ones (both \mention{god fuck} hits are exclamations before a separate imprecative, as in \mention{oh my god, fuck you}).

Fourth, speech has both PP frames, \mention{damn it to hell} once and \mention{for} V-ing three times in three conversations (\mention{bless him for carrying}, \mention{damn you for being busy}, \mention{bless him for coming on TV}), and no expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} with a lexical NP (0 of 17).

Rates per million words, computed from raw strings in both corpora, show how subtitles differ from speech \citep{kilgarriff2001}. \mention{Bless you} runs at the same rate in both (7.8~pmw in Spoken BNC2014, 7.5 in the TV Corpus), \mention{fuck it} and \mention{sod it} run higher in speech (11.7 vs.\ 5.7; 1.4 vs.\ 0.4), and \mention{fuck you} and \mention{damn it} far higher in drama (7.9 vs.\ 21.1; 11.8 vs.\ 61.9). At TV rates, Spoken BNC2014 would hold 2--3 tokens of \mention{for} V-ing, and it has 3. The corresponding figure for \mention{to hell} is about 3 (it has 1), a difference too small to mean much. For the euphemisms of \mention{fuck}, the sampled TV rate (0.47~pmw) would predict about 5, but 39 of the 68 sampled tokens come from one franchise, \mention{frak} in \emph{Battlestar Galactica} and \emph{Caprica}. Scaled without it, the expectation falls to about 2, so the zero says little \citep{kilgarriff2005}.
