\begin{abstract}
English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives~-- \mention{Damn the bell!}, \mention{Screw you for putting me in this position!}, \mention{Bless his little cotton socks!}~-- pair obligatory accusative pronouns and open lexical NPs with restricted, non-finite syntax, but grammatical accounts rest largely on introspection over \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}. Mapping thirteen core heads, six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms, and archaic \mention{confound} across 19.95~million locally audited words, 11.83~million words of unscripted British speech (Spoken BNC2014), and a 2.225-billion-word subtitle and web search space (TV Corpus, GloWbE) reveals graded, lineage-specific verbal retention rather than a uniform category. Open lexical-NP complementation separates these heads from primary interjections (with no lexical-NP complements verified for expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}), and non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{me} rule out imperatives and a uniform speaker-subject reduced-performative analysis. Inside the family, extensions split sharply by lineage: goal \mention{to hell} concentrates overwhelmingly on the \mention{damn} family, whereas evaluative \mention{for} V-ing spans six heads alongside retaliatory particle verbs and praise/blame frames; overt \mention{God} clusters on theological heads and civic \mention{bless}; and euphemisms mirror their sources only partially across formulaic, passive, and spoken frames. Observational corpus data still underdetermines lexicalist, constructional, and interjection-phrase analyses, but any adequate grammar has to capture this head-specific profile.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} imprecatives; benedictives; construction grammar; defective verbs; English syntax

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

English contains a pragmatically vivid family of expressions without an overt grammatical subject in which an expressive head combines directly with a noun phrase (which may itself carry a determinative, a genitive pronoun, or be pronominal) and, optionally, a prepositional phrase. Attested examples span secular taboo maledictives, theological curses, affectionate benedictives, and mild minced oaths, as illustrated in (\ref{ex:intro-paradigm}).

\ea\label{ex:intro-paradigm}
\ea \mention{Damn the bell!} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{H85})
\ex \mention{Fuck the doctors.} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{CCW})
\ex \mention{Sod the choir!} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{KPU})
\ex \mention{Bugger the rest!} \hfill (CABNC \texttt{KB7})
\ex \mention{Screw the Cabinet and the rest.} \hfill (OANC \texttt{Slate})
\ex \mention{Bless you for helping me out with the Watsons.} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{G0Y})
\ex \mention{Oh, darn the luck.} \hfill (TV Corpus, \emph{The Simpsons})
\z
\z

Since \textcite[3--7]{quang1971} analysed \mention{fuck you} and \mention{damn him} as \enquote{English sentences without overt grammatical subject} headed by \enquote{quasi-verbs}, grammarians have struggled to locate this family in English syntax. \textcite[236, 944, 1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002} distinguish restricted verbal optatives from \mention{Damn these mosquitoes!}, tentatively treating the latter as an exceptional interjection phrase and doubting an understood subject. \textcite[852--853]{quirk1985} list \mention{Damn} (\mention{it}), \mention{Bugger} (\mention{it}), and \mention{Fuck} (\mention{it}) among expletive formulae alongside interjections.

Semanticists and corpus linguists approach the same forms from different angles. Formal semanticists model expressive and use-conditional meaning \citep{potts2007,gutzmann2015,gutzmann2019} or the social-relation semantics of profane quasi-verbs while setting morphosyntax aside \citep[4]{portner2026}. Corpus studies treat them as taboo verbs, euphemisms, or interactives \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,napolihoeksema2009,ljung2011,heine2023interactives}.

Almost every grammatical discussion relies on introspection over \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}: focusing on \mention{damn}/\mention{bless} invites \mention{God}-ellipsis, \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw} invites defective verbs, and \mention{damn it} invites interjections. None has been tested across all thirteen core heads (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}), six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms (\mention{frak}, \mention{feck}, \mention{eff}, \mention{frig}, \mention{fudge}, \mention{frick}), archaic \mention{confound}, and \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}.

Previous debates compress four questions into a \enquote{defective verb} vs.\ \enquote{construction} binary: (1) separation from primary interjections, \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}, imperatives, and uniform reduced performatives; (2) which extensions (\mention{God}, \mention{to hell}, \mention{for} V-ing, coordination) generalize vs.\ stay lexically anchored; (3) complement-NP openness vs.\ head-slot recruitment and partial euphemistic correspondence; and (4) how live analyses accommodate these uneven distributions.

This study addresses those questions across 19.95M locally audited words (CABNC, BNC Baby XML, OANC), 11.83M words of contemporary unscripted British speech (Spoken BNC2014), and a 2.225B-word subtitle and web search space (TV Corpus, GloWbE; plus exploratory COCA checks). Its aim is comparative grammar with explicit uncertainty bounds: mapping what's attested, what's lexically concentrated, and what observational data does and doesn't settle.

\subsection{Morphosyntactic diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The thirteen core heads come from three historical strata: theological optatives (\mention{damn}, \mention{bless}, \mention{curse}, \mention{blast}), secular taboo or violent verbs (\mention{fuck}, \mention{screw}, \mention{sod}, \mention{bugger}, \mention{hang}), and minced oaths (\mention{drat}, \mention{darn}, \mention{dash}, \mention{dang}).\footnote{Historically, \mention{drat} contracts (\mention{Go})\mention{d rot}, \mention{darn} and \mention{dang} are phonological euphemisms for \mention{damn}, and \mention{dash} entered early nineteenth-century British English as a mild euphemism associated with the printed dash replacing \mention{damn} \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,ljung2011}.} Across these strata, the subjectless NP pattern displays seven grammatical properties, summarized in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}), where asterisks mark ungrammaticality \emph{on the subjectless imprecative reading}.

\ea\label{ex:diagnostics}
\ea Obligatory accusative pronoun form on case-marking pronouns (descriptive, compatible with default case): \mention{Damn him!} / \mention{Fuck them!} / \ungram{\mention{Damn he!}} / \ungram{\mention{Fuck they!}}
\ex Open lexical NP complementation: \mention{Screw the Times!} / \mention{Sod the bar codes!} / \mention{Oh, darn the luck!} / \mention{Confound that man!}
\ex Non-reflexive second- and first-person objects (unlike imperatives and a uniform speaker-subject reduced-performative analysis): \mention{Fuck you!} / \mention{Damn you all!} (vs.\ imperative \mention{Fuck yourself!}); \mention{Fuck me!} / \mention{Bugger me!} (vs.\ \ungram{\mention{I fuck me!}}, predicting \mention{myself})
\ex Absence of tense, agreement, aspect, imperative \mention{do}-negation, and \mention{please} (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{\mention{Damned the bell!}} / \ungram{\mention{Don't damn him!}} (imprec.) / \odd{\mention{Please sod the choir!}}
\ex Absence of ordinary overt subjects (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{\mention{I sod the choir!}} / \ungram{\mention{You fuck you!}} (vs.\ imperative \mention{You fuck off!})
\ex No eventive passive preserving imprecative force (\ungram{\mention{The rules are being fucked by me!}}, imprec.), alongside productive bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!})
\ex Split modification: rejection of event-level manner/time adverbials on \mention{fuck}/\mention{damn} (\ungram{\mention{Fuck him quickly!}}, \ungram{\mention{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}}; \citealp[5--6]{quang1971}) alongside evaluative \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{Fuck her for killing herself!}), dismissal-scope temporal adjuncts (\mention{Screw the Times \dots\ for the moment}), and lexically restricted \mention{to hell} (\mention{Damn him to hell!}).
\z
\z

Three points sharpen (\ref{ex:diagnostics}). First, accusative form in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}a) doesn't alone prove verbal Case assignment: English assigns \term{default accusative case} outside nominative positions, including non-verbal expressive small clauses (\mention{Poor me!}, \mention{Silly me!}; \citealp{schutze2001,pottsroeper2006}) and expressive \mention{yay me!} / \mention{boo him!} (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}). What separates (\ref{ex:diagnostics}) from primary interjections (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}; \citealp{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015}) and from \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} in these searches~-- where no lexical-NP complements of expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} were verified (Sections~\ref{sec:tv-profile} and~\ref{sec:bnc2014})~-- is the combination of obligatory accusative pronouns and open determined or genitive-determiner lexical NPs, alongside evaluative \mention{for} V-ing across six heads.

Second, non-reflexive objects in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}c) yield two subject-omission diagnostics. Non-reflexive second-person \mention{you} separates subjectless imprecatives from ordinary imperatives \citep[3]{quang1971,pinker2007,portner2026}, whose implicit addressee subject binds a reflexive (\mention{Fuck yourself!}). Parallel reasoning applies to non-reflexive first-person \mention{me} (\mention{Fuck me!}, \mention{Bugger me!}, attested in Tier~1 and \texttt{T3}): a uniform speaker-subject reduced-performative analysis (\mention{Thank you} $<$ \mention{I thank you}) predicts reflexive \mention{myself} when the speaker is the target, ruling out that uniform analysis across secular heads. Literal verbs remain grammatical in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}d--f) on non-imprecative readings (e.g.\ liturgical \mention{Don't damn him!}), while \odd{\mention{Please sod the choir!}} reflects register clash rather than pure syntactic ill-formedness.

Third, (\ref{ex:diagnostics}f--g) qualify two classical claims. Bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!}) confirm that some heads retain passive-participle syntax outside the active subjectless frame (Section~\ref{sec:minced}). And while \textcite[5--6]{quang1971} cited the rejection of event-level manner/time adverbials (\ungram{\mention{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}}) against verbhood, Sections~\ref{sec:tier1} and \ref{sec:dissoc} document productive evaluative-scope modifiers~-- especially \mention{for} V-ing and frame-level \mention{for the moment}.

\subsection{Live analytical accounts}
\label{sec:four-positions}

Four analytical perspectives frame the literature. Under optative and reduced-performative subject omission (Position~A), \mention{bless} and \mention{damn} derive historically from third-person \mention{God}-optatives \citep{huddleston2002,ljung2011,grosz2012}, while reduced performatives (\mention{Thank you} $<$ \mention{I thank you}) explain non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{for} V-ing on verbs of judging \citep{fillmore1971}. Formula-conditioned optative omission remains viable locally on \mention{bless} and \mention{damn}, and \mention{thank you for} V-ing supplies a comparative anchor for reason modification (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}), whereas non-reflexive \mention{me} rules out a uniform speaker-subject reduced-performative analysis across secular heads.

A second perspective contrasts primary interjections with complement-retaining interjection phrases (Position~B). Interjection theories separate \term{primary interjections} (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}), which don't govern NP complements, from \term{secondary interjections} (\mention{damn!}, \mention{fuck!}, \mention{bless!}) recruited from lexical words \citep{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015,heine2023interactives}. Open lexical NPs exceed unanalysed frozen chunks (\mention{damn it}, \mention{dash it}), while a complement-retaining interjection phrase \citep[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002,amekawilkins2006} remains a live rival.

A third perspective is modernized lexicalist defective-entry grammar (Position~C). From \textcite[7]{quang1971} (\texttt{Epithet $\to$ Quasi-verb NP}) to modern lexicalist frameworks \citep{mullerwechsler2014}, each living head carries a defective lexical entry selecting an NP in a subjectless frame. Missing finite forms don't refute defectiveness: paradigm gaps define defective verbs (compare \mention{beware}, \mention{betide}), and a developed lexicalist entry can incorporate partial verbal paradigms, horizontal euphemistic links, default accusative case \citep{schutze2001}, and evaluative \mention{for} V-ing \citep{fillmore1971}.

A fourth perspective is a usage-based constructional network (Position~D). Modern Construction Grammar \citep{fillmore1988mechanisms,goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2003,croft2001,bybee2010,diessel2019,goldberg2019,schmid2020} posits a structured network pairing schematic [X\textsubscript{expr} NP] with lexically anchored sub-constructions (civic \mention{God bless} NP, \mention{damn} NP \mention{to hell}, British \mention{damn and blast}) and horizontal euphemistic links~-- converging closely with enriched lexicalism (cf.\ Sign-Based Construction Grammar, \citealp{sag2012sign}).

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ===========================================================================

No single corpus pairs exhaustive manual verification with the multi-billion-word scale needed for rare frames, so I combine two complementary tiers.

\subsection{Tier 1: Locally audited British and American corpora (19.95M words)}
\label{sec:tier1-methods}

Tier~1 extracts exhaustively across CABNC \citep{albert2015cabnc} (1,860 spoken BNC1994 CHAT files; 2,054,817 tokens), BNC Baby XML \citep{burnard2007bncbaby} (182 CLAWS5-tagged files; 4,001,787 tokens), and OANC \citep{ide2008oanc} (8,824 American files; 14,449,791 tokens). Removing 12 overlapping BNC Baby \texttt{dem} files (559,070 tokens) leaves 10,854 disjoint files and 19,947,325 deduplicated tokens (5.50M UK; 14.45M US).

Inspecting 200 CABNC files showed 0/200 contain timed Jeffersonian pauses; BNC1994 \texttt{<s>} unit-initial capitals (\mention{Ouch My foot}) are reported descriptively because clause boundaries enter coding. CABNC and OANC target closed-class NP starters (\mention{it}, pronouns, determinatives, genitive pronouns, quantifiers), while BNC Baby admits any NP-starting CLAWS5 tag to calibrate missed heads. All 596 target candidates and 148 primary-interjection candidates (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}) were manually adjudicated at line level ($\text{raw} = \text{verified} + \text{God} + \text{excluded}$).

\subsection{Tier 2: Subtitle, web, and contemporary spoken corpora}
\label{sec:tier2-methods}

Tier~2 pairs a 2.225-billion-word subtitle and web search space (plus exploratory COCA checks; \citealp{davies2020coca}) with an 11.83-million-word unscripted British benchmark (exact queries in the Appendix).

First, the TV Corpus \citep{davies2019tv} (325M words, 1950--2018) runs 31 queries (\texttt{T1a}--\texttt{T8b}, \texttt{F1a}--\texttt{F5c}, \texttt{C1a}--\texttt{C3b}, \texttt{D1}--\texttt{D4}); because default KWIC displays cap at 100 nonrandom lines (or fewer, e.g.\ 85/104 in \texttt{T7a}, 72/99 in \texttt{T7b}), audits pair inspected lines with deterministic undisplayed-hit bounds.

Second, GloWbE \citep{davies2013glowbe,daviesfuchs2015} (1.9B words, 20 varieties) runs \texttt{G1}--\texttt{G4} and \texttt{C4} on minced oaths; without concordances, \texttt{G1}/\texttt{G2} serve strictly as lexically screened \term{candidate attestations}.

Third, Spoken BNC2014 \citep{loveEtAl2017SpokenBNC2014} (11.83M tokens, 2012--2016) runs eight Sketch Engine CQL queries (\texttt{preloaded/bnc2014\_spoken\_tt3}, \texttt{B2-1a}--\texttt{B2-5b}) calibrating subtitle patterns against spontaneous speech.

\subsection{Coding taxonomy, exclusions, and quantitative reporting}
\label{sec:coding}

Verified Tier-1 subjectless tokens fall into seven mutually exclusive classes (with phraseological priority for routine \mention{bless you} over generic \mention{you}, and genitive body-part/soul formulae over residual full NPs): \mention{it}-targets (fused \mention{damn it}, \mention{fuck it}, \mention{sod it}, plus referential \mention{And bless it}); routine \mention{bless you}; second-person \mention{you}; case-marking accusative pronouns (\mention{fuck me}, \mention{bugger him}); fused determiner-head demonstratives (\mention{sod this}); genitive body-part/soul formulae (\mention{bless his heart}); and remaining open full NPs (\mention{Damn the bell!}). Overt \mention{God} + HEAD + NP optatives are tallied separately.

Exclusions remove literal or finite transitive verbs (\mention{to hang the curtains}, \mention{to darn her own stockings}), negative quantifier idioms (\mention{bugger/sod/fuck all}), standalone interjections before a new clause (\mention{Fuck. That would have been amusing}), metalinguistic citations (\emph{Verbatim}), proper-noun homonyms (\emph{Dash}), and West African ditransitive \mention{dash} (\enquote{give/tip}) in GloWbE.

Because tokens cluster by file, formula, and series, and Tier-2 KWICs reflect nonrandom display, I omit independent-token \emph{p}-values \citep{kilgarriff2005}. Instead, I report counts, explicit denominators, document/conversation dispersion, per-million-word (pmw) calibrations with \textcite{kilgarriff2009}'s simple-maths ratio ($(\text{pmw}_1 + 1)/(\text{pmw}_2 + 1)$), undisplayed-hit bounds, and~-- strictly as descriptive sparse-event association and power guides, not \emph{p}-values~-- \textcite{dunning1993}'s log-likelihood ratio ($G^2$) and exact binomial 95\% zero upper bounds (noting clustering widens token-level bounds). Zero cells remain nonattestations unless a baseline fixes expected counts.

This graded profile defines what any analysis has to capture, while observational corpus data still underdetermines the choice among three live accounts: a complement-retaining interjection phrase \citep[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002,amekawilkins2006}, modernized lexicalist defective entries \citep{quang1971,mullerwechsler2014}, and a structured usage-based constructional network \citep{goldberg2003,goldberg2019,croft2001,bybee2010,diessel2019,schmid2020}, paralleling sign-based continua \citep{sag2012sign}. Both enriched lexical entries and anchored construction networks can store head-specific retention, default accusative case \citep{schutze2001}, evaluative \mention{for} V-ing \citep{fillmore1971}, and horizontal euphemistic links. Testing acceptability across \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}, preposition-headed dismissals (\mention{to hell with} X, \mention{down with} X), and inanimate \mention{for} V-ing remains a productive experimental agenda, while this comparative map stands independently.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Four bounds remain: Spoken BNC2014 leaves a matched American spoken benchmark for future work; GloWbE lacked concordances (leaving \texttt{G1}/\texttt{G2} screened candidates and excluding \mention{dash} NP); a single coder adjudicated lines, so multi-coder reliability on a stratified sample would strengthen borderline distinctions; and corpus counts measure attestation rather than graded acceptability, leaving inanimate \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{Damn this printer for jamming again!}) and attachment diagnostics for judgment and audio work.

Within those bounds, this comparative map establishes the profile of English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives across 19.95M local words, 11.83M words of British speech, and a 2.225B-word subtitle and web search space. Open NPs separate them from primary interjections (with no expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} lexical NPs verified); non-reflexive \mention{you}/\mention{me} rule out imperatives and uniform speaker-subject performatives; base-rate conditioning confirms that \mention{to hell} avoids \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw}/\mention{bless} while \mention{for} V-ing spans six heads (matching TV pmw rates in conversation); overt \mention{God} clusters on theological heads and civic \mention{bless}; and euphemisms show partial correspondence alongside formulaic, passive, and spoken limits. Together, these asymmetries show graded, lineage-specific verbal retention across heads.
