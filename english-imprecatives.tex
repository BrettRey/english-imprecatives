\begin{abstract}
English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives (\mention{Damn the bell!}, \mention{Screw you for putting me in this position!}, \mention{Bless his little cotton socks!}) combine an expressive head with an accusative pronoun or an open lexical NP, but grammatical accounts of them rest largely on introspection about \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}. This study maps thirteen heads, six euphemisms of \mention{fuck}, and archaic \mention{confound} across 20 million words of British and American English with every candidate checked by hand, 12 million words of unscripted British speech, and 2.2 billion words of subtitles and web text. Taking open NPs separates these heads from primary interjections such as \mention{ouch}, and non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{me} rule out a single understood addressee or speaker subject for the family as a whole. Within the family, heads differ in which verbal properties they keep. \mention{Damn him to hell} is almost confined to \mention{damn}, whereas \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{Bless him for trying}) occurs with six heads; overt \mention{God} clusters on \mention{damn} and \mention{bless}; and euphemisms such as \mention{darn} share some of their sources' frames but not others. Corpus data can't choose among lexicalist, constructional and interjection-phrase analyses, but any adequate grammar has to capture this head-by-head profile.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} imprecatives; benedictives; construction grammar; defective verbs; English syntax

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

English contains a pragmatically vivid family of expressions without an overt grammatical subject in which an expressive head combines directly with a noun phrase and, optionally, a prepositional phrase. Attested examples span secular taboo maledictives, theological curses, affectionate benedictives, and mild minced oaths, as illustrated in (\ref{ex:intro-paradigm}).

\ea\label{ex:intro-paradigm}
\ea \mention{Damn the bell!} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{H85})
\ex \mention{Fuck the doctors.} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{CCW})
\ex \mention{Sod the choir!} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{KPU})
\ex \mention{Bugger the rest!} \hfill (CABNC \texttt{KB7})
\ex \mention{Bless you for helping me out with the Watsons.} \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{G0Y})
\ex \mention{Oh, darn the luck.} \hfill (TV Corpus, \emph{The Simpsons})
\z
\z

Since \textcite[3--7]{quang1971} analysed \mention{fuck you} and \mention{damn him} as \enquote{English sentences without overt grammatical subject} headed by \enquote{quasi-verbs}, grammarians have struggled to locate this family in English syntax. \textcite[236, 944, 1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002} distinguish restricted verbal optatives from \mention{Damn these mosquitoes!}, tentatively treating the latter as an exceptional interjection phrase and doubting an understood subject. \textcite[852--853]{quirk1985} list \mention{Damn} (\mention{it}), \mention{Bugger} (\mention{it}), and \mention{Fuck} (\mention{it}) among expletive formulae alongside interjections.

Semanticists and corpus linguists approach the same forms from different angles. Formal semanticists model expressive and use-conditional meaning \citep{potts2007,gutzmann2015,gutzmann2019} or the social-relation semantics of profane quasi-verbs while setting morphosyntax aside \citep[4]{portner2026}. Corpus studies treat them as taboo verbs, euphemisms, or interactives \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,napolihoeksema2009,ljung2011,heine2023interactives}.

Almost every grammatical discussion relies on introspection over \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}: focusing on \mention{damn}/\mention{bless} invites \mention{God}-ellipsis, \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw} invites defective verbs, and \mention{damn it} invites interjections. None has been tested across all thirteen core heads (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}), six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms (\mention{frak}, \mention{feck}, \mention{eff}, \mention{frig}, \mention{fudge}, \mention{frick}), archaic \mention{confound}, and \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}.

Previous debates compress four questions into a \enquote{defective verb} vs.\ \enquote{construction} binary: (1) separation from primary interjections, \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}, imperatives, and uniform reduced performatives; (2) which extensions (\mention{God}, \mention{to hell}, \mention{for} V-ing, coordination) generalize vs.\ stay lexically anchored; (3) complement-NP openness vs.\ which words fill the head slot, including partial euphemistic correspondence; and (4) how live analyses accommodate these uneven distributions.

This study addresses those questions across 20M words checked in full (CABNC, BNC Baby XML, OANC), 12M words of unscripted British speech (Spoken BNC2014), and 2.2B words of subtitles and web text (TV Corpus, GloWbE), with a few exploratory COCA checks. Its aim is comparative grammar with explicit uncertainty bounds: mapping what's attested, what's lexically concentrated, and what observational data does and doesn't settle.

\section{Grammatical profile, pragmatic subtypes, and analytical positions}

\subsection{Morphosyntactic diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The thirteen core heads come from three historical strata: theological optatives (\mention{damn}, \mention{bless}, \mention{curse}, \mention{blast}), secular taboo or violent verbs (\mention{fuck}, \mention{screw}, \mention{sod}, \mention{bugger}, \mention{hang}), and minced oaths (\mention{drat}, \mention{darn}, \mention{dash}, \mention{dang}).\footnote{Historically, \mention{drat} contracts (\mention{Go})\mention{d rot}, \mention{darn} and \mention{dang} are phonological euphemisms for \mention{damn}, and \mention{dash} entered early nineteenth-century British English as a mild euphemism associated with the printed dash replacing \mention{damn} \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,ljung2011}.} Across these strata, the subjectless NP pattern displays seven grammatical properties, summarized in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}), where asterisks mark ungrammaticality \emph{on the subjectless imprecative reading}.

\ea\label{ex:diagnostics}
\ea Obligatory accusative pronoun form on case-marking pronouns: \mention{Damn him!} / \mention{Fuck them!} / \ungram{\mention{Damn he!}} / \ungram{\mention{Fuck they!}}
\ex Open lexical NP complementation: \mention{Screw the Times!} / \mention{Sod the bar codes!} / \mention{Oh, darn the luck!} / \mention{Confound that man!}
\ex Non-reflexive second- and first-person objects: \mention{Fuck you!} / \mention{Damn you all!} (vs.\ imperative \mention{Fuck yourself!}); \mention{Damn me for agreeing to it!} / \mention{Fuck me!} (vs.\ \ungram{\mention{I damn me for agreeing to it!}}, predicting \mention{myself})
\ex Absence of tense, agreement, aspect, imperative \mention{do}-negation, and \mention{please} (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{\mention{Damned the bell!}} / \ungram{\mention{Don't damn him!}} (imprec.) / \odd{\mention{Please sod the choir!}}
\ex Absence of ordinary overt subjects (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{\mention{I sod the choir!}} / \ungram{\mention{You fuck you!}} (vs.\ imperative \mention{You fuck off!})
\ex No eventive passive preserving imprecative force (\ungram{\mention{The rules are being fucked by me!}}, imprec.), alongside productive bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!})
\ex Split modification: rejection of event-level manner and time adjuncts on \mention{fuck}/\mention{damn} (\ungram{\mention{Fuck him quickly!}}, \ungram{\mention{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}}; \citealp[5--6]{quang1971}) alongside evaluative \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{Fuck her for killing herself!}), dismissal-scope temporal adjuncts (\mention{Screw the Times \dots\ for the moment}), and lexically restricted \mention{to hell} (\mention{Damn him to hell!}).
\z
\z

Three points sharpen (\ref{ex:diagnostics}). First, accusative form in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}a) doesn't alone prove verbal Case assignment: English assigns \term{default accusative case} outside nominative positions, including non-verbal expressive small clauses (\mention{Poor me!}, \mention{Silly me!}; \citealp{schutze2001,pottsroeper2006}) and expressive \mention{yay me!} / \mention{boo him!} (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}). What separates (\ref{ex:diagnostics}) from primary interjections (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}; \citealp{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015}) and from expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} (Sections~\ref{sec:tv-profile} and~\ref{sec:bnc2014}) is the combination of obligatory accusative pronouns and open lexical NPs, alongside evaluative \mention{for} V-ing across six heads.

Second, non-reflexive objects in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}c) test for an understood subject. An addressee subject would bind a reflexive, as in the imperative \mention{Fuck yourself!} \citep[3]{quang1971,pinker2007,portner2026}, so non-reflexive \mention{you} excludes one for the \mention{you} forms. A speaker subject, as in a reduced performative (\mention{Thank you} $<$ \mention{I thank you}), would require \mention{myself} when the speaker is the target, so non-reflexive \mention{me} in self-curses (\mention{Damn me for agreeing to it}, \emph{Babylon 5}; \mention{Fuck me for giving a shit}, \emph{Shameless}; both TV Corpus) excludes one for those forms. Neither test excludes a third-person subject, and with someone present a \mention{me} form could still have an addressee subject (Section~\ref{sec:ruled-out}). Literal verbs remain grammatical in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}d--f) on non-imprecative readings (e.g.\ liturgical \mention{Don't damn him!}), while \odd{\mention{Please sod the choir!}} reflects register clash rather than pure syntactic ill-formedness.

Third, (\ref{ex:diagnostics}f--g) qualify two classical claims. Bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!}) confirm that some heads retain passive-participle syntax outside the active subjectless frame (Section~\ref{sec:minced}). And while \textcite[5--6]{quang1971} cited the rejection of event-level manner and time adjuncts (\ungram{\mention{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}}) against verbhood, Sections~\ref{sec:tier1} and \ref{sec:dissoc} document productive evaluative-scope modifiers~-- especially \mention{for} V-ing and frame-level \mention{for the moment}.

\subsection{Pragmatic and semantic subtypes}
\label{sec:subtypes}

Grouping all HEAD + NP sequences under one speech act hides functional differences that bear on their extensions. Dismissals typically target inanimate things or institutions (\mention{Damn the bell!}, \mention{Screw the committee}), whereas targeted curses and acts of praise or thanks evaluate a responsible referent (\mention{Fuck her for killing herself}, \mention{Bless him for trying}), and it's these that take a reason PP in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}). Routine uses (\mention{Damn it!}, \mention{Bless you!}) have no referential target at all.

\subsection{Live analytical accounts}
\label{sec:four-positions}

Four analytical perspectives frame the literature. Under optative and reduced-performative subject omission (Position~A), \mention{bless} and \mention{damn} derive historically from third-person \mention{God}-optatives \citep{huddleston2002,ljung2011,grosz2012}, while reduced performatives (\mention{Thank you} $<$ \mention{I thank you}) explain non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{for} V-ing on verbs of judging \citep{fillmore1971}. Formula-conditioned optative omission remains viable locally on \mention{bless} and \mention{damn}, and \mention{thank you for} V-ing supplies a comparative anchor for reason modification (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}), whereas non-reflexive \mention{me} excludes a speaker subject for the \mention{me} forms (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

A second perspective contrasts primary interjections with complement-retaining interjection phrases (Position~B). Interjection theories separate \term{primary interjections} (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}), which don't govern NP complements, from \term{secondary interjections} (\mention{damn!}, \mention{fuck!}, \mention{bless!}) recruited from lexical words \citep{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015,heine2023interactives}. Open lexical NPs exceed unanalysed frozen chunks (\mention{damn it}, \mention{dash it}), while a complement-retaining interjection phrase \citep[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002,amekawilkins2006} remains a live rival.

A third perspective is modernized lexicalist defective-entry grammar (Position~C). From \textcite[7]{quang1971} (\texttt{Epithet $\to$ Quasi-verb NP}) to modern lexicalist frameworks \citep{mullerwechsler2014}, each living head carries a defective lexical entry selecting an NP in a subjectless frame. Missing finite forms don't refute defectiveness: paradigm gaps define defective verbs (compare \mention{beware}, \mention{betide}), and a developed lexicalist entry can incorporate partial verbal paradigms, horizontal euphemistic links, default accusative case \citep{schutze2001}, and evaluative \mention{for} V-ing \citep{fillmore1971}.

A fourth perspective is a usage-based constructional network (Position~D). Modern Construction Grammar \citep{fillmore1988mechanisms,goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2003,croft2001,bybee2010,diessel2019,goldberg2019,schmid2020} posits a structured network pairing schematic [X\textsubscript{expr} NP] with lexically anchored sub-constructions (civic \mention{God bless} NP, \mention{damn} NP \mention{to hell}, British \mention{damn and blast}) and horizontal euphemistic links~-- converging closely with enriched lexicalism (cf.\ Sign-Based Construction Grammar, \citealp{sag2012sign}).

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ===========================================================================

No single corpus pairs exhaustive manual checking with the scale that rare frames need, so I combine two tiers.

\subsection{Tier 1: British and American corpora checked in full (20M words)}
\label{sec:tier1-methods}

Tier~1 searches three corpora in full: CABNC \citep{albert2015cabnc} (2.05M tokens of British conversation from BNC1994), BNC Baby XML \citep{burnard2007bncbaby} (4.0M tokens, CLAWS5-tagged), and OANC \citep{ide2008oanc} (14.45M tokens of American English). Removing 12 overlapping BNC Baby files leaves 19,947,325 distinct tokens (5.50M UK; 14.45M US).

The CABNC and OANC searches require the NP to begin with a closed-class word (\mention{it}, a pronoun, a determinative, a genitive pronoun, or a quantifier). The BNC Baby search accepts any NP-initial tag, as a check on what the closed-class searches miss. I checked every one of the 596 candidates by hand, along with 148 candidates for six primary interjections (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}). CABNC transcripts carry no timed pauses (none in 200 files inspected), so the BNC1994 transcribers' sentence-initial capitals (\mention{Ouch My foot}) serve only as a rough cue to unit boundaries.

\subsection{Tier 2: Subtitle, web, and contemporary spoken corpora}
\label{sec:tier2-methods}

Tier~2 adds scale. The TV Corpus \citep{davies2019tv} holds 325M words of subtitles (1950--2018) and GloWbE \citep{davies2013glowbe,daviesfuchs2015} 1.9B words of web text from 20 varieties; a few exploratory checks use COCA \citep{davies2020coca}. Spoken BNC2014 \citep{loveEtAl2017SpokenBNC2014} (11.83M words of unscripted British conversation, 2012--2016) tests whether the subtitle patterns hold in speech. Supplementary Table~S1 lists every query with the ID used in the tables (\texttt{T7a}, for example, is HEAD + pronoun + \mention{to hell} in the TV Corpus), and the replication archive gives full tallies for each.

The TV Corpus interface displays at most 100 concordance lines per query by default, and not a random 100. Where a query returns more hits than it displays, I audit the displayed lines and report bounds that hold however the undisplayed lines turn out (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}). GloWbE was searched without concordance lines, so its hits count only as \term{candidate attestations}, screened by meaning.

\subsection{Coding and quantitative reporting}
\label{sec:coding}

Verified Tier-1 tokens are coded by target into the seven classes of Table~\ref{tab:tier1-pooled}: \mention{it} (\mention{damn it}, \mention{sod it}); routine \mention{bless you}; other \mention{you}; other accusative pronouns (\mention{fuck me}, \mention{bugger him}); demonstratives (\mention{sod this}); genitive body-part and soul formulae (\mention{bless his heart}); and open full NPs (\mention{Damn the bell!}). Routine \mention{bless you} takes priority over \mention{you}, and body-part formulae over full NPs. Optatives with overt \mention{God} are counted separately.

Exclusions remove ordinary transitive verbs (\mention{to hang the curtains}, \mention{to darn her own stockings}), negative quantifier idioms (\mention{bugger/sod/fuck all}), standalone interjections before a new clause (\mention{Fuck. That would have been amusing}), citations of the word, proper nouns (\emph{Dash}), and West African ditransitive \mention{dash} (\enquote{give, tip}) in GloWbE.

Tokens cluster by file, formula and series, and the displayed concordance lines aren't random samples, so I report no \emph{p}-values \citep{kilgarriff2005}. I report counts with their denominators, the number of files, shows or conversations they come from, and rates per million words (pmw). Two further figures serve only as descriptive guides: \textcite{dunning1993}'s log-likelihood ratio ($G^2$), for how surprising a zero is on a large base, and the exact binomial 95\% upper bound on a zero, for how little a zero on a small base says (0 in 17 is compatible with a true rate as high as 16\%). A zero counts as a nonattestation unless a baseline fixes how many tokens were expected.

\subsection{Primary interjections take no NPs}
\label{sec:cabnc-boundaries}

The six primary interjections give 148 candidates followed by an NP-initial word, and none is a direct NP complement. Every hit is the subject of a following clause (\mention{Whoops, you've dropped your pencil}), a nursery formula (\mention{oops a daisy}), a lexical verb (\mention{to wow us}), or a detached fragment (\mention{Ouch. My foot}; \mention{Ugh, the prep}; compare \ungram{\mention{Ouch him!}}, \ungram{\mention{Ugh them!}}).

The transcribers' capitals point the same way. None of 80 verified subjectless tokens in CABNC has a capital between head and NP, against 9 of 23 primary-interjection sequences (\mention{Ouch My foot}) and 4 of 7 standalone \mention{damn} or \mention{fuck} tokens (\mention{Fuck. That would have been amusing}).

\subsection{Missed heads, regional skew, and overt \mentionhead{God}}
\label{sec:tier1-regional}

The broader BNC Baby search recovered three bare nouns or proper names that the closed-class searches miss (\mention{Damn McIllvanney}, \mention{Oh, screw Jesus Christ}, \mention{night, bless mummy and dad}). It also exposed an ambiguity: \mention{Damn DTs.} is either a subjectless imprecative or prenominal \mention{damn} (compare \mention{Damn rubbish.}; \citealp[35 n.\ 29]{portner2026}). That ambiguity is why the large-corpus queries require an overt determinative, genitive pronoun or accusative pronoun.

Tier~1 also shows regional skew, though the registers differ too much for a controlled UK--US comparison. \mention{Sod} and \mention{bugger} each occur 20 times in the British data and once in the American. Overt \mention{God} already concentrates on the theological heads: it occurs in 20 of 102 \mention{bless} tokens and 3 of 36 \mention{damn} tokens (all \mention{God damn it}), and in none of 105 tokens of the other seven attested heads.

To check whether the thirteen heads miss productive ones, I ranked every clause-initial bare word before an accusative pronoun in BNC Baby by $G^2$. Apart from reduced performatives (\mention{thank}, \mention{excuse}), ordinary imperatives and discourse particles, \mention{bless} ranks highest among imprecative and benedictive heads (20 tokens in 8 files), beside \mention{goodness me!}. Searching for heads proposed in the literature turns up peripheral British \mention{blow} (\mention{ooh, blow this for a lark}, \mention{blow me!}), \mention{stuff} (\mention{stuff it!}, \mention{stuff this!}) and \mention{stone} (\mention{stone the crows}). The thirteen heads are a sampling frame, not a closed inventory.

\subsection{Overt \mentionhead{God}}
\label{sec:god-split}

The TV Corpus confirms the Tier-1 pattern at scale. Of 4,084 tokens of \mention{God} + HEAD + NP, 4,031 have \mention{damn} or \mention{bless}. Setting aside \mention{it}-formulae and routine \mention{God bless you}, \mention{bless} keeps 686 and \mention{damn} 140, against 8 for \mention{curse}, 1 for \mention{fuck} (\mention{God fuck you}), and none for the other nine heads.

The minced heads take \mention{God} only in fixed \mention{it}-formulae. All 39 \mention{God dang/darn} tokens are on \mention{it}, and so are all 135 \mention{God/Gosh/Dog + darn/dang} strings in a follow-up search limited to pronouns (\mention{gosh darn it}, \mention{god dang it}).

Within \mention{bless}, overt \mention{God} depends on the expression. Across eight frequent strings (Supplementary Table~S2), \mention{God} precedes 80 of 86 tokens of four civic formulae (\mention{the United States}, \mention{the King}) but only 12 of 272 tokens of four idioms (\mention{your heart}, \mention{my soul}). The strings are illustrative rather than a sample, and the counts without \mention{God} are raw (they include song lyrics and surprise \mention{bless my soul}). Even so, 93\% against 4\% points to conditioning by expression rather than a uniform rule; optative accounts can allow omission conditioned by formula \citep{grosz2012}.

Neighbouring frames show where \mention{for} V-ing occurs. It's frequent after \mention{thank you} (\mention{thank you for coming}) and occurs with the praise and blame formulae \mention{shame on}, \mention{good for} and \mention{kudos to}. Without an audited count of ordinary transitive \mention{fuck} and \mention{screw}, though, the contrast between 37 subjectless tokens, 4 with particle verbs, and none with finite or infinitival verbs shows where the frame is attested, not that the construction alone licenses it. Whether inanimate targets accept it (\mention{Damn this printer for jamming again!}) is a question for judgment studies, and \mention{Eff you all to H!} (Section~\ref{sec:minced}, example~\ref{ex:f3-examples}c) shows that \mention{to hell} can extend by analogy.

\subsection{Unscripted speech: Spoken BNC2014}
\label{sec:bnc2014}

To check the subtitle patterns against unscripted speech, I repeated the core searches in Spoken BNC2014.

First, the living heads dominate: \mention{bless} (502 hits), \mention{fuck} (457), \mention{damn} (167), \mention{screw} (50), \mention{bugger} (27) and \mention{sod} (26). Auditing 100 of 237 lines with a determinative or genitive verifies 48 direct tokens, mostly demonstratives (\mention{sod this for a lark}) and \mention{fuck my life}, with five open full NPs (\mention{Fuck this boat club}, \mention{damn those vampires}, \mention{bless that poor girl who was acting}). The archaic and minced heads barely occur: of 22 hits, 4 are imprecatives (\mention{dash it all}, \mention{curse the world}, and \mention{darn it} twice), and there's a single \mention{oh confound it}.

Second, none of the 11 hits for the euphemisms of \mention{fuck} is an imprecative (9 are literal \mention{fudge}), although \mention{fuck it} (138) and \mention{fuck you} (94) are frequent. Their subtitle use may reflect broadcast censorship and scripted invention, which this data can't separate.

Third, overt \mention{God} again goes with the theological heads: 10 \mention{god damn it} and 5 verified \mention{god bless} optatives, and no secular ones (both \mention{god fuck} hits are exclamations before a separate imprecative, as in \mention{oh my god, fuck you}).

Fourth, speech has both PP frames, \mention{damn it to hell} once and \mention{for} V-ing three times in three conversations (\mention{bless him for carrying}, \mention{damn you for being busy}, \mention{bless him for coming on TV}), and no expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} with a lexical NP (0 of 17).

Rates per million words show how subtitles differ from speech \citep{kilgarriff2001}. \mention{Bless you} runs at the same rate in both (7.8~pmw in Spoken BNC2014, 7.5 in the TV Corpus), \mention{fuck it} and \mention{sod it} run higher in speech (11.7 vs.\ 5.7; 1.4 vs.\ 0.4), and \mention{fuck you} and \mention{damn it} far higher in drama (7.9 vs.\ 21.1; 11.8 vs.\ 61.9). At TV rates, Spoken BNC2014 would hold 2--3 tokens of \mention{for} V-ing, and it has 3. The corresponding figures are about 3 for \mention{to hell} (it has 1) and 2--7 for the euphemisms of \mention{fuck} (it has none).

\section{Discussion: Convergence, divergence, and productivity}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{What the diagnostics exclude, and the head slot}
\label{sec:ruled-out}

The results constrain the accounts in Section~\ref{sec:four-positions}. Non-reflexive \mention{you} excludes an understood addressee subject for the \mention{you} forms, though a speaker subject remains possible (\mention{I damn you}). Non-reflexive \mention{me} in self-curses (\mention{Damn me for agreeing to it}) excludes a speaker subject, though with someone present an addressee subject remains possible (\mention{(you) damn me}). So no single addressee or speaker subject fits the whole family.

A mixed analysis, with a speaker subject for the \mention{you} forms and an addressee subject for the \mention{me} forms, fails wherever self-curses occur with no one to address, since there's then no addressee and binding still excludes the speaker. First-person narration supplies such cases. A narrator who has just \enquote{found myself alone} continues \mention{Tricked. Damn him. And damn me for falling for it.} (COCA, Kelley Armstrong, \emph{Broken}, 2006), and \mention{Damn me for shooting my mouth off that night} (COCA, \emph{Fantasy \& Science Fiction}, 2007) is addressed to no one in the scene. Only a third-person subject or none fits them, and for \mention{damn} a third-person source is available, since \mention{God damn} is frequent (Section~\ref{sec:god-split}).

For \mention{fuck} it isn't. To my ear, \mention{Fuck me for being an idiot!}, muttered alone, is possible, and overt \mention{God} is almost absent on the secular heads, with a single \mention{God fuck you} in the TV Corpus. If that judgment holds, \mention{fuck} has no understood subject in this use (Positions~B--D). A speaker alone may address fate or God, but an addressee of that kind is the third-person optative source under another name. Bleached \mention{Fuck me!} is natural alone too (on dropping something heavy, say), which excludes an addressee subject, but its \mention{me} may not be referential, so it says nothing about a speaker subject.

Open lexical NPs separate these heads from the six primary interjections searched. Other interjection-like heads take dependents (\mention{shame on you}, \mention{yay me}), so a complement-taking interjection phrase (Position~B) remains open. Finally, \mention{confound}, \mention{blow}, \mention{stuff} and the euphemisms show which words fill the head slot \citep{mcenery2006,napolihoeksema2009,ljung2011}. Whether a head is a recent recruit or a survival is a diachronic question these counts can't answer, though \mention{confound}'s concentration in period drama suggests survival.

Theological \mention{damn} keeps the fullest verbal-optative profile, while \mention{bless} keeps civic \mention{God} and \mention{for} V-ing without \mention{to hell}. Secular \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw} pair open NPs and productive \mention{for} V-ing (joining retaliatory \mention{fuck up}/\mention{screw over}) with zero \mention{to hell}, while British \mention{sod}/\mention{bugger} retain open NPs and minor NP \mention{be buggered} (their PP zeros rest on only 75 raw pronoun strings, so they carry little weight). Minced \mention{darn}/\mention{dang} enter open NPs selectively while copying fixed formulae string-by-string (\mention{God dang/darn it}, \mention{I'll be darned!}), alongside archaic \mention{confound} and the subtitle euphemisms of \mention{fuck}.

This graded profile defines what any analysis has to capture, while observational corpus data still underdetermines the choice among three live accounts: a complement-retaining interjection phrase \citep[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002,amekawilkins2006}, modernized lexicalist defective entries \citep{quang1971,mullerwechsler2014}, and a structured usage-based constructional network \citep{goldberg2003,goldberg2019,croft2001,bybee2010,diessel2019,schmid2020}, paralleling sign-based continua \citep{sag2012sign}. Both enriched lexical entries and anchored construction networks can store head-specific retention, default accusative case \citep{schutze2001}, evaluative \mention{for} V-ing \citep{fillmore1971}, and horizontal euphemistic links. Testing acceptability across \mention{yay}/\mention{boo}, preposition-headed dismissals (\mention{to hell with} X, \mention{down with} X), and inanimate \mention{for} V-ing remains a productive experimental agenda.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Four bounds remain: Spoken BNC2014 leaves a matched American spoken benchmark for future work; GloWbE lacked concordances (leaving its NP hits as screened candidates and excluding \mention{dash} + NP); a single coder adjudicated lines, so multi-coder reliability on a stratified sample would strengthen borderline distinctions; and corpus counts measure attestation rather than graded acceptability, leaving inanimate \mention{for} V-ing (\mention{Damn this printer for jamming again!}) and attachment diagnostics for judgment and audio work.

Within those bounds, this comparative map establishes the profile of English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives across 20M words checked in full, 12M words of British speech, and 2.2B words of subtitles and web text. Open NPs separate them from primary interjections (with no expressive \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} lexical NPs verified); non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{me} rule out a single understood addressee or speaker subject for the family as a whole; base-rate conditioning confirms that \mention{to hell} avoids \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw}/\mention{bless} while \mention{for} V-ing spans six heads (matching TV pmw rates in conversation); overt \mention{God} clusters on theological heads and civic \mention{bless}; and euphemisms show partial correspondence alongside formulaic, passive, and spoken limits. Together, these asymmetries show graded, lineage-specific verbal retention across heads.
