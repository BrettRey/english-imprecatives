\begin{abstract}
English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives~-- expressions without an overt subject in which an expressive head combines directly with an NP, such as \mention{Damn the bell!}, \mention{Screw you for putting me in this position!}, and \mention{Bless his little cotton socks!}~-- pair obligatory accusative pronouns and open lexical NPs with defective verbal syntax. Previous accounts rely mostly on introspection over \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}. This corpus-based grammatical study maps thirteen core heads plus six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms across 19.95~million deduplicated words of locally audited British and American corpora (CABNC, BNC Baby XML, OANC) and 2.225~billion words of television subtitles and web English (TV Corpus, GloWbE), plus an exploratory COCA check. Five empirical findings organize the family. First, these heads separate from primary interjections (0/148 local direct-NP and 0/53 audited TV accusative-pronoun complements), preserve non-reflexive second-person \mention{you} \citep[3]{quang1971}, and show internal reflexive binding in \mention{for V-ing} clauses (\mention{Fuck her for killing herself}). Second, secular \mention{fuck} and \mention{screw} host evaluative \mention{for V-ing} inside this frame (37 audited tokens across adjacent and quantified pronouns) while recording 0 bare-transitive finite/infinitival hits outside it, joining praise/blame and reduced-performative frames (\mention{thank you}, \mention{shame on}, \mention{good for}, \mention{silly me}) and contrasting sharply with \mention{damn}-concentrated \mention{to hell}. Third, overt \mention{God} is virtually absent from secular heads and shows sharp expression-specific concentration across eight illustrative \mention{bless} phrases. Fourth, euphemistic mirroring replicates across \mention{damn}- and \mention{fuck}-variants alongside sharp non-inheritances (0 finite curse-sense \mention{darn/dang/drat} against 297 same-corpus GloWbE controls; formulaic \mention{I'll be darned}; 135/135 minced \mention{God/Gosh} frozen on \mention{it}). Together, these asymmetries establish the grammatical constraints any lexicalist, constructional, or interjection-phrase account needs to meet.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} imprecatives, benedictives, construction grammar, defective verbs, English syntax

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

English contains a pragmatically vivid family of expressions without an overt grammatical subject in which an expressive head combines directly with a noun phrase (which may itself be determined, possessive, or pronominal) and, optionally, a prepositional phrase. Attested examples span secular taboo maledictives, theological curses, affectionate benedictives, and mild minced oaths, as illustrated in (\ref{ex:intro-paradigm}).

\ea\label{ex:intro-paradigm}
\ea Damn the bell! \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{H85})
\ex Fuck the doctors. \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{CCW})
\ex Sod the choir! \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{KPU})
\ex Bugger the rest! \hfill (CABNC \texttt{KB7})
\ex Screw the Cabinet and the rest. \hfill (OANC \texttt{Slate})
\ex Bless you for helping me out with the Watsons. \hfill (BNC Baby \texttt{G0Y})
\ex Darn you for raising us so well. \hfill (TV Corpus, \emph{Good Luck Charlie})
\z
\z

Since \textcite[3--7]{quang1971} analysed \mention{fuck you} and \mention{damn him} as \enquote{English sentences without overt grammatical subject} headed by \enquote{quasi-verbs}, grammarians have struggled to locate this family in English syntax. \textcite[236, 944, 1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002} distinguish restricted verbal optatives from \mention{Damn these mosquitoes!}, tentatively treating the latter as an exceptional interjection phrase and doubting an understood subject; \textcite[852--853]{quirk1985} list \mention{Damn (it)}, \mention{Bugger (it)}, and \mention{Fuck (it)} among expletive formulae alongside interjections; formal semanticists model expressive and use-conditional meaning \citep{potts2007,gutzmann2015,gutzmann2019} or the social-relation semantics of profane quasi-verbs while setting morphosyntax aside \citep[4]{portner2026}; and corpus studies treat them as taboo verbs, euphemisms, or interactives \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,napolihoeksema2009,ljung2011,heine2023interactives}.

Almost every grammatical discussion has relied on introspection over \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck} without mapping the wider paradigm in attested English. Concentrating on \mention{damn} or \mention{bless} invites a \mention{God}-ellipsis account; concentrating on \mention{fuck} or \mention{screw} invites a defective-verb account; concentrating on frozen \mention{damn it} invites an interjection account. None of those extrapolations has been tested across all thirteen core heads (spanning theological, secular taboo, and minced-oath strata; Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}) plus six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms (\mention{frak}, \mention{feck}, \mention{eff}, \mention{frig}, \mention{fudge}, \mention{frick}).

Previous debates also compress four distinct questions into a binary choice between \enquote{defective verb} and \enquote{construction}: (1) how the family separates from primary interjections, imperatives, and frozen formulae; (2) which extensions (\mention{God}-subjects, \mention{to hell}, \mention{for V-ing}, coordination) generalize across heads versus stay lexically anchored; (3) how complement-NP openness differs from head-slot recruitment and partial euphemistic inheritance; and (4) what constraints these asymmetries place on live lexicalist, constructional, reduced-performative, and interjection-phrase analyses.

This corpus-based grammatical study addresses those questions across 19.95~million deduplicated words of locally audited British and American corpora (CABNC, BNC Baby XML, OANC) paired with 2.225~billion words of television subtitles and web English (TV Corpus, GloWbE; plus an exploratory COCA check). Rather than forcing a premature knockout between architectural labels, it maps the division between shared syntax, head-specific extensions, and partial euphemistic inheritance that any adequate grammar of English needs to encode.

\subsection{Morphosyntactic diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The thirteen core heads represent three historical strata: theological optatives (\mention{damn}, \mention{bless}, \mention{curse}, \mention{blast}), secular taboo or violent verbs (\mention{fuck}, \mention{screw}, \mention{sod}, \mention{bugger}, \mention{hang}), and minced oaths (\mention{drat}, \mention{darn}, \mention{dash}, \mention{dang}).\footnote{Historically, \mention{drat} contracts \mention{(Go)d rot}, \mention{darn} and \mention{dang} are phonological euphemisms for \mention{damn}, and \mention{dash} entered early nineteenth-century British English as a mild euphemism associated with the printed dash replacing \mention{damn} \citep{mcenery2006,allanburridge2006,ljung2011}.} Across these strata, the subjectless NP pattern displays seven grammatical properties, summarized in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}), where asterisks mark ungrammaticality \emph{on the subjectless imprecative reading}.

\ea\label{ex:diagnostics}
\ea Obligatory accusative pronoun form (on case-marking pronouns): Damn him! / Fuck them! / \ungram{Damn he!} / \ungram{Fuck they!}
\ex Open lexical NP complementation: Screw the \emph{Times}! / Sod the bar codes! / Oh, darn the luck! / Frak the Gods!
\ex Non-reflexive second-person object (unlike imperatives): Fuck you! / Damn you all! vs.\ imperative Fuck yourself! (\ungram{Fuck you!} as an ordinary reflexive imperative)
\ex Absence of tense, agreement, aspect, imperative \mention{do}-negation, and \mention{please} (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{Damned the bell!} / \ungram{Don't damn him!} (imprec.) / \#Please sod the choir!
\ex Absence of ordinary overt subjects (on the imprecative reading): \ungram{I sod the choir!} / \ungram{You fuck you!} (vs.\ imperative \mention{You fuck off!})
\ex No eventive passive preserving imprecative force (\ungram{The rules are being fucked by me!}, imprec.), alongside productive bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!})
\ex Split modification: rejection of event-level manner/time adverbials on \mention{fuck}/\mention{damn} (\ungram{Fuck him quickly!}, \ungram{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}; \citealp[5--6]{quang1971}) alongside evaluative \mention{for V-ing} (\mention{Fuck her for killing herself!}), dismissal-scope temporal adjuncts (\mention{Screw the Times \dots\ for the moment}), and lexically restricted \mention{to hell} (\mention{Damn him to hell!}).
\z
\z

Three points sharpen (\ref{ex:diagnostics}). First, accusative form in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}a) doesn't alone prove verbal Case assignment: English assigns \term{default accusative case} outside nominative positions, including non-verbal expressive small clauses like \mention{Poor me!} and \mention{Silly me!} \citep{schutze2001,pottsroeper2006}. What separates (\ref{ex:diagnostics}) from primary interjections (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}; \citealp{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015}) is the bundle of obligatory accusative pronominal targets (\ungram{Ouch him!}), open determined/possessive lexical NPs, and evaluative \mention{for V-ing}.

Second, non-reflexive \mention{you} in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}c) separates subjectless imprecatives from ordinary imperatives \citep[3]{quang1971,pinker2007,portner2026}: imperatives carry an implicit addressee subject binding a reflexive (\mention{Fuck yourself!}), whereas subjectless imprecatives take number-neutral non-reflexive \mention{you} (\mention{Damn you all!}, \mention{Eff you both!}). Literal verbs remain grammatical in (\ref{ex:diagnostics}d--f) on non-imprecative readings (e.g.\ liturgical \mention{Don't damn him!}), while \mention{\#Please sod the choir!} reflects register clash rather than pure syntactic ill-formedness.

Third, (\ref{ex:diagnostics}f--g) qualify two classical claims. Bare-subjunctive passive dismissals (\mention{The law be damned!}, \mention{Sony be buggered!}) confirm that some heads retain passive-participle syntax outside the active subjectless frame (Section~\ref{sec:minced}). And while \textcite[5--6]{quang1971} cited the rejection of event-level manner/time adverbials (\ungram{Damn those irregular verbs tomorrow!}) against verbhood, Sections~\ref{sec:tier1} and \ref{sec:dissoc} document productive evaluative-scope modifiers~-- especially \mention{for V-ing} and frame-level \mention{for the moment}.

\subsection{Live analytical accounts and descriptive baselines}
\label{sec:four-positions}

To clarify what the corpus asymmetries constrain, I organize the literature around four analytical perspectives:

\paragraph{Position A: Optative and reduced-performative subject omission.}
Historically, \mention{bless} and \mention{damn} derive from third-person optative clauses with subject \mention{God} \citep{huddleston2002,ljung2011,grosz2012}; a parallel reduced-performative model (\mention{Thank you} $<$ \mention{I thank you}) explains non-reflexive \mention{you} and \mention{for V-ing} on verbs of judging \citep{fillmore1971}. Whereas an unconditioned ellipsis baseline fails across secular heads (\mention{fuck}, \mention{screw}, \mention{sod}, \mention{bugger}), formula-conditioned optative omission remains viable locally on \mention{bless} and \mention{damn}, and \mention{thank you for V-ing} supplies a major comparative anchor for reason modification (Section~\ref{sec:dissoc}).

\paragraph{Position B: Primary interjections versus complement-retaining interjection phrases.}
Interjection theories separate \term{primary interjections} (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}), which lack lexical homonyms and don't govern NP complements, from \term{secondary interjections} (\mention{damn!}, \mention{fuck!}, \mention{bless!}), which recruit lexical words into expressive utterances \citep{ameka1992,amekawilkins2006,meinard2015,heine2023interactives}. Open lexical NPs exceed a \emph{frozen-formula} view (treating \mention{damn it}, \mention{dash it}, and \mention{bless you} as unanalysed chunks), while a \emph{complement-retaining} interjection phrase~-- \textcite[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002}'s tentative analysis of \mention{Damn these mosquitoes!}, alongside argument-taking interjections discussed by \textcite{amekawilkins2006}~-- remains a live descriptive rival.

\paragraph{Position C: Modernized lexicalist defective-entry grammar.}
In the lexicalist tradition from \textcite[7]{quang1971} (\texttt{Epithet $\to$ Quasi-verb NP}) to lexicalist argument-structure frameworks \citep{mullerwechsler2014}, each living head carries a sense-specific defective lexical entry selecting an NP target in a subjectless frame. \textbf{Showing that a head lacks ordinary finite or past-tense forms doesn't refute a defective-verb analysis}: paradigm gaps are the defining property of defectiveness (compare English \mention{beware} and \mention{betide}, though subjectless imprecatives go further by excluding ordinary subjects across all cells). A developed lexicalist account can incorporate partial verbal paradigms, horizontal euphemistic lexical links (\mention{damn} $\leftrightarrow$ \mention{darn/dang}, \mention{fuck} $\leftrightarrow$ \mention{frak/feck/eff/frig/fudge/frick}), English default accusative case \citep{schutze2001}, and judgment-frame or evaluative \mention{for V-ing} modifiers \citep{fillmore1971}.

\paragraph{Position D: Usage-based constructional network.}
Under Construction Grammar \citep{fillmore1988mechanisms,goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2003,croft2001}, the pairing of an expressive head and its NP target forms a stored constructional pattern. Unlike a lexically insensitive schematic baseline predicting uniform extensions across heads, modern usage-based construction grammar \citep{bybee2010,diessel2019,goldberg2019,schmid2020} posits a \emph{structured network} combining a schematic subjectless expressive pattern \mention{[X\textsubscript{expr} NP]}, lexically anchored sub-constructions (civic \mention{God bless NP}, \mention{damn NP to hell}, British \mention{damn and blast}), and horizontal euphemistic links~-- a representation that converges closely with an enriched lexicalist network (cf.\ Sign-Based Construction Grammar, \citealp{sag2012sign}). Section~\ref{sec:discussion} evaluates how these live accounts handle the corpus diagnostics in Table~\ref{tab:verdict}.

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% ===========================================================================

No single corpus combines exhaustive, line-level manual verification over POS-tagged and spoken transcripts with the multi-billion-word scale required to observe low-frequency frames such as minced oaths with lexical NPs, lexical-NP \mention{for V-ing}, or \mention{fuck}-euphemisms. I pair two complementary tiers of corpus data.

\subsection{Tier 1: Locally audited British and American corpora (19.95M words)}
\label{sec:tier1-methods}

Tier~1 executes exhaustive extraction under stated query rules across three local corpora:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CABNC} \citep{albert2015cabnc}: 1,860 TalkBank CHAT files of spoken demographic British English from BNC1994 (246,783 utterances; 2,054,817 word tokens).
\item \textbf{BNC Baby XML} \citep{burnard2007bncbaby}: 182 CLAWS5 POS-tagged XML files spanning demographic spoken (\texttt{dem}), fiction (\texttt{fic}), newspaper (\texttt{news}), and academic prose (\texttt{aca}), totalling 4,001,787 tokens.
\item \textbf{OANC} \citep{ide2008oanc}: 8,824 plain-text files of American spoken (Switchboard, Charlotte) and written English (\emph{Slate}, \emph{Verbatim}, fiction, non-fiction, technical prose), totalling 14,449,791 word tokens.
\end{itemize}

Three safeguards govern Tier~1. First, 12 overlapping BNC Baby \texttt{dem} files (559,070 tokens; listed in the replication archive) duplicate CABNC and are removed, yielding 10,854 disjoint files and \textbf{19,947,325 deduplicated word tokens} (5.50M UK; 14.45M US). Second, 0/200 inspected CABNC \texttt{.cha} files contain timed Jeffersonian pauses; they preserve BNC1994 \texttt{<s>} unit-initial capitalization (\mention{Ouch My foot}), reported descriptively only because clause boundaries enter coding. Third, CABNC and OANC queries target closed-class NP starters (\mention{it}, personal pronouns, determinatives, genitive pronouns in determiner function, quantifiers), whereas BNC Baby's CLAWS5 tags accept \emph{any} NP-starting tag~-- including \texttt{NP0} and bare nouns~-- to calibrate what closed-class filters miss. All 596 target candidates and 148 primary-interjection candidates (\mention{ouch}, \mention{oops}, \mention{ugh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{whoops}, \mention{yuck}) were manually adjudicated at line level, with Python verifying bookkeeping ($\text{raw} = \text{verified} + \text{God} + \text{excluded}$).

\subsection{Tier 2: Large-scale subtitle and web corpora (2.225B words)}
\label{sec:tier2-methods}

Tier~2 scales across 2.225~billion words in two Mark Davies corpora plus an exploratory COCA check \citep{davies2020coca} (exact query strings in Table~\ref{tab:appendix-queries}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The TV Corpus} \citep{davies2019tv}: 325~million words of six-variety television \emph{subtitles} (1950--2018). Twenty-six structured queries cover baseline profiles (\texttt{T1a}--\texttt{T8b}), evaluative/euphemism/participial follow-ups (\texttt{F1a}--\texttt{F5c}), and targeted controls (\texttt{C1a}--\texttt{C3b}). Because the interface displays up to 100 KWIC lines in default order rather than a random sample, KWIC precision characterizes only inspected lines.
\item \textbf{GloWbE} \citep{davies2013glowbe,daviesfuchs2015}: 1.9~billion words of web English across 20 national varieties. Six queries (\texttt{G1}--\texttt{G4} plus same-corpus positive control \texttt{C4}) probe minced oaths (\mention{drat}, \mention{darn}, \mention{dash}, \mention{dang}) in lexical-NP, accusative-pronoun, attributive/adverbial, and past-tense frames. Because the interface returned country tables without concordances under access limits during collection, \texttt{G1} and \texttt{G2} serve strictly as lexically screened \term{candidate attestations}, while \texttt{G4} pairs with same-corpus \texttt{C4} and KWIC-audited \texttt{F5b}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coding taxonomy, exclusions, and statistical protocol}
\label{sec:coding}

Verified Tier-1 subjectless tokens are coded into seven mutually exclusive formal categories (prioritizing routine \mention{bless you} over generic \mention{you}): \mention{it}-targets (fused \mention{damn it}, \mention{fuck it}, \mention{sod it}, plus referential \mention{And bless it}); routine \mention{bless you} (sneeze, parting, gratitude); second-person \mention{you} (\mention{fuck you}, \mention{damn you}); case-marking accusative pronouns (\mention{fuck me}, \mention{bugger him}, \mention{bless her}); fused determiner-head demonstratives (\mention{sod this}, \mention{bugger that}); possessive body-part/soul formulae (\mention{bless his heart}, \mention{bless his cotton socks}); and full lexical NPs (\mention{Damn the bell!}, \mention{Screw the Cabinet}). Overt \mention{God + HEAD + NP} optatives are tallied separately.

Exclusions remove literal or finite transitive verbs (\mention{to hang the curtains}, \mention{won't screw it up}, \mention{to darn her own stockings}); negative quantifier idioms (\mention{bugger/sod/fuck all}); standalone interjections preceding a new clause (\mention{Fuck. That would have been amusing}); metalinguistic citations (\emph{Verbatim} glosses); proper-noun homonyms (UK retailer \emph{Dash}); and West African English ditransitive \mention{dash} (\enquote{give/tip}) in GloWbE. Because tokens cluster within files, formulae, and series, two-sided Fisher's exact tests are reported only on matched-denominator tables alongside document/series dispersion as descriptive strength indicators rather than assuming full token independence.

\subsection{Grammatical constraints across live analyses}
\label{sec:ruled-out}

Table~\ref{tab:verdict} establishes the empirical constraints any grammar of English has to encode: expression-conditioned overt \mention{God} alongside frozen minced \mention{God/Gosh} on \mention{it}; \mention{damn}-anchored \mention{to hell} versus six-head \mention{for V-ing} (including secular \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw} with 37 subjectless tokens against 0 bare-transitive finite hits); and open lexical NPs with reflexive-bound \mention{for V-ing} exceeding primary interjections and frozen chunks. Three live analyses survive these constraints: a \emph{complement-retaining secondary-interjection phrase} \citep[1361 n.\ 70]{huddleston2002,amekawilkins2006}, a \emph{lexicalist defective-entry grammar} (Position~C), and a \emph{structured constructional network} (Position~D), alongside local formula-conditioned optatives on \mention{bless}/\mention{damn} and the reduced-performative \mention{thank you} anchor (\texttt{C2a}) for reason modification.

\subsection{Complement openness versus head-slot recruitment}
\label{sec:productivity}

Separating \textbf{complement-NP openness} from \textbf{head-slot recruitment} clarifies the paradigm. The \textbf{complement NP slot} admits definite, demonstrative, possessive, coordinated, and proper-name NPs across living heads (24 Tier-1 full-NP tokens plus audited Tier-2 concordances). The \textbf{head slot}, by contrast, excludes the six primary interjections across 345~million searched words (0/148 Tier-1; 0/53 audited TV) and expands via (i) \textbf{taboo verbal recruitment} \citep{mcenery2006,napolihoeksema2009,ljung2011} and (ii) \textbf{euphemistic mirroring} across both \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck} without developing finite curse inflection.

\subsection{Convergence of lexicalist and constructionist accounts}
\label{sec:convergence}

The central theoretical result is that \textbf{observational corpus data underdetermine the choice between Position~C (a modernized lexicalist defective-entry grammar) and structured Position~D (a usage-based constructional network)}~-- much as sign-based frameworks place lexical entries and phrasal constructions on a continuum \citep{sag2012sign}. Under \textbf{Position~C} \citep{quang1971,mullerwechsler2014}, each living head carries a defective lexical entry restricted to a subjectless expressive clause (\texttt{Epithet $\to$ Quasi-verb NP}): missing finite forms (\texttt{G4}, \texttt{F5b}) follow from defectiveness; formulaic \mention{be darned/danged} (\texttt{F5a}) fit retained participial cells; accusative pronouns receive default case \citep{schutze2001}; \mention{for V-ing} reflects judgment-frame Reason modification \citep{fillmore1971} extended analogically onto \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw}; and lexical asymmetries and euphemistic links reside in the lexicon. Under \textbf{structured Position~D} \citep{goldberg2003,goldberg2019,croft2001,bybee2010,diessel2019,schmid2020}, speakers generalize a schematic construction \mention{[X\textsubscript{expr} NP]} whose judgment-predication semantics directly explains why non-judgment verbs like \mention{fuck} and \mention{screw} host \mention{for V-ing} inside this frame, linked vertically to lexically anchored sub-constructions (civic \mention{God bless NP}, \mention{damn NP to hell}, British \mention{damn and blast}) and horizontally to euphemistic allolexes.

Even experimental coercion of novel non-verbal heads wouldn't automatically separate Position~C from Position~D without fixing auxiliary assumptions, since lexicalist grammars permit productive lexical rules \citep{mullerwechsler2014} while usage-based networks can restrict head slots to entrenched items \citep{goldberg2019}. Testing how speakers extend subjectless accusative complementation and \mention{for V-ing} beyond established verbal and euphemistic models~-- including comparing \mention{yay}/\mention{boo} and preposition-headed dismissals (\mention{to hell with X}, \mention{down with X})~-- remains a productive experimental agenda, while the corpus contribution stands independently: any viable grammar has to encode both the shared subjectless NP/judgment-Reason profile and the sharp lexical, phraseological, and euphemistic asymmetries documented here.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Four methodological bounds remain. First, TV subtitles boost archaic British formulae through period drama (\mention{dash it}, \mention{damn and blast}, \mention{God rot}) and euphemisms through broadcast censorship (\mention{frak}, \mention{eff}, \mention{fudge}); pairing distinct-series dispersion with spontaneous speech such as the Spoken BNC2014 \citep{loveEtAl2017SpokenBNC2014} would sharpen the boundary between living and period-bound heads. Second, GloWbE lacked concordances during collection, leaving \texttt{G1}/\texttt{G2} as screened candidates and excluding context-free \mention{dash NP}. Third, a single coder performed Tier-1 and Tier-2 adjudication; multi-coder reliability on a stratified sample would strengthen borderline distinctions. Fourth, corpus counts measure attestation rather than graded acceptability; targeted judgment studies~-- testing inanimate-target \mention{for V-ing} (\mention{Damn this printer for jamming again!}), \mention{to hell} vs.\ \mention{for V-ing}, and extension beyond verbal/euphemistic heads~-- remain the natural next step.

Within those bounds, this comparative map clarifies English subjectless imprecatives and benedictives across thirteen core heads and six \mention{fuck}-euphemisms: an open NP complement slot, non-reflexive second-person syntax, reflexive-bound judgment-frame \mention{for V-ing} adopted by secular \mention{fuck}/\mention{screw} despite zero bare-transitive finite hits, sharp head- and expression-specific asymmetries on \mention{to hell} and overt \mention{God}, and partial euphemistic inheritance across both \mention{damn} and \mention{fuck}.
